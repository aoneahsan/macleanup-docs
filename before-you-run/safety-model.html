Safety model

macleanup is safe by default and it is still a tool that runs rm -rf and sudo on your machine. Both halves of that sentence are true and neither cancels the other. This page states exactly what the design guarantees, and where the guarantee ends and your judgement begins.

The six rules​

1. Destructive operations confirm before running​

Every destructive section prompts [y/N] before it acts. --yes auto-confirms those prompts; the typed-yes gate on section 14 is independent and is not affected by --yes.

2. --dry-run never deletes anything​

Every destructive call routes through a helper that no-ops in dry mode. See Preview with --dry-run.

3. App data and apps are moved to the Trash​

Sections 12 and 21 use Finder to move orphaned data and applications to the Trash, so they stay recoverable until it is emptied. Section 24 does the same for large stale files. If Finder fails, the affected item stays in place.

4. Sudo sections are skipped, never silently bypassed​

Sections needing root ask for sudo. If sudo is unavailable — or you passed --no-sudo — the section is skipped and reported, never half-run. Sudo-requiring sections: 6, 7 (system logs), 9 (system updates), 11, 13, 14, 20, 25 (system LaunchAgents), and 27 (only the optional system font-cache clear).

5. The deepest sections never run unattended by accident​

Six sections are gated: 6 (system caches as root), 10 (Empty Trash), 11 (Time Machine snapshots), 14 (/private/var/folders wipe), 21 (app uninstall) and 24 (large stale files → Trash).

  • They are never part of --all — not even --all --yes.
  • To run one unattended via --only or --profile, you need --i-understand-deep in addition to --yes. --yes alone is refused, with a warning, and the section is skipped.
  • --dry-run previews are always permitted: they delete nothing, although normal logs and reports may be written to disk.
  • Section 14 additionally requires a typed yes.

The gate exists so that a one-character typo in a section number cannot trigger an irreversible operation.

6. Every run is logged​

A dated log lands in ~/.macleanup/logs/, and the sections that produce lists write dated .txt reports to ~/.macleanup/reports/. Both live in your home directory, outside any npx cache, and survive every invocation. See Reports and logs.

The two-condition rule​

Introduced in 4.3.3, this governs candidate selection in four sections that can remove non-cache data: 12 (orphaned app data), 21 (idle apps), 23 (stale build artefacts) and 24 (large unused files).

An item qualifies only when both hold:

  1. It is not in use by any installed software or tool. The per-section detection decides this — no matching installed app for orphan data, no last-used signal and not currently running for an idle app.
  2. It meets that section's age threshold — --idle-days for 12 and 23, --threshold for 21, or --large-file-days for 24. All default to 100 days. Sections 12, 23 and 24 check atime and mtime; section 21 uses available app last-use signals and skips apps without a usable signal.

Condition 2 is what makes the rule work in practice. A node_modules your editor reads for autocomplete keeps a recent access time and survives. A Gradle distribution you invoke monthly survives. Lowering an age threshold widens the candidate list; review it before confirming a deletion.

Sections 16 and 17 are different

For iOS backups (16) and Xcode archives (17), --idle-days only highlights idle items with an [idle ≥Nd] marker. There is no automatic delete gate — both are interactive-only and will delete any item you confirm, recent or not.

Idle-app detection, and why it under-flags​

Section 21 decides an app is idle using five signals in priority order: Spotlight kMDItemLastUsedDate, Saved Application State mtime, Container mtime, Preferences plist mtime, and Spotlight kMDItemUseCount.

An app with no usable signal is skipped. The tool deliberately under-flags: missing a stale app is cheap, uninstalling a needed one is not.

The section 23 allowlist​

CRITICAL_HOME_DIRS is a hard refusal list. Section 23 will not enter toolchain-manager directories such as ~/.bun, ~/.pnpm-store, ~/.local/share/pnpm, ~/.npm-packages and ~/.volta, regardless of how old their contents look.

Section 23 also refuses to scan without roots. If none of the standard dev folders exist (~/Projects, ~/Code, ~/Developer, ~/dev, ~/repos, ~/work, ~/Documents, ~/Desktop, ~/Downloads) and you passed no --scan-roots, it errors out and asks for one rather than falling back to scanning your entire home directory.

Both rails exist because v4.3.0 lacked them and broke people's global tooling. That incident, and how to recover from it, is documented in full in Recovery. It is the strongest argument on this site for running --dry-run first.

Add your own protected trees with the repeatable --exclude-path:

npx macleanup --only 23 \
  --exclude-path "$HOME/Code/active-project" \
  --exclude-path "$HOME/Code/client-work" --dry-run

Confirmation levels, from weakest to strongest​

LevelWhereBypassed by
Batch-mode section promptMost destructive sections--yes
Per-item interactive selectionSections 12, 16, 17, 21, 23, 24Running the section in batch mode
Deep-section gateSections 6, 10, 11, 14, 21, 24--yes and --i-understand-deep together
Typed yesSection 14Nothing — always required

Privacy: no telemetry​

The script makes no network calls by default. The Node launcher only spawns bash — no analytics, no crash reporting, no phone-home. Logs and reports never leave ~/.macleanup/.

The one exception is opt-in: --check-update makes a single unauthenticated GET to https://registry.npmjs.org/macleanup/latest to compare versions. It sends no cleanup paths or reports. npm still receives normal request metadata, such as your IP address. The check times out in seconds and never blocks the run. --feedback and --report-issue open your mail client or a pre-filled GitHub issue for you to review and submit — nothing is sent automatically.

Where the guarantees stop​

The rules above bound what macleanup does. They do not bound the consequences of a cleanup you asked for:

  • There is no undo. No restore point, no transaction log, no macleanup --undo. The Trash is the only built-in second chance, and only for sections 12, 21 and 24.
  • Deleting the right file at the wrong time still hurts. A stale node_modules is only reinstallable while the lockfile resolves and the registry still has those versions.
  • Heuristics are heuristics. "Orphaned", "idle" and "stale" are informed guesses about your intent. Review the interactive lists.
  • --yes transfers the judgement to you. It is the correct flag for a cron job you have already validated with --dry-run, and the wrong flag for a command you are trying for the first time.
  • Third-party apps can hide data anywhere. An uninstall removes the known locations, not every location.

Back up before any cleanup tool runs. A pinned Time Machine snapshot or a tmutil snapshot is the last line of defence that actually works.
